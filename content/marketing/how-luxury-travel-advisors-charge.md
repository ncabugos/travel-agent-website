# How Luxury Travel Advisors Charge: Planning Fees, Commissions, and Retainers

Luxury travel advisors are paid in three ways: commission from suppliers such as hotels and cruise lines, planning fees charged directly to the client, and annual retainers for clients who travel often. Most established luxury practices combine commission with a planning fee. The fee covers the design work that commission does not pay for, and it filters for clients who value that work.

Fees are the subject advisors are most uneasy discussing and clients are most curious about. Here is how each model works, where each one fits, and how to present your fee so it reads as a standard of service rather than a hurdle.

## Key Takeaways

- Commission is paid by suppliers, not clients, and it is still the base of most luxury practices.
- A planning fee pays for the research, design, and changes that happen before anything is booked, including trips that never book.
- Retainers suit clients who travel several times a year and want priority access to you.
- How you present the fee matters as much as the amount. Explain what it covers, state it plainly, and put it where a prospect will find it before the first call.

## How do travel advisors get paid by commission?

When a client books a hotel, cruise, or tour through an advisor, the supplier pays the advisor's agency a commission on the booking. The client usually pays the same rate they would pay booking direct, and through a consortium they often receive added amenities on top. For independent advisors, the commission is shared between the advisor and their host agency according to their agreement.

Commission works well for straightforward bookings. It works poorly for complex trips with many components, long planning cycles, or suppliers that pay little or nothing, such as some private guides, restaurants, and villa owners. It also pays nothing when a client takes your itinerary and books elsewhere, or decides not to travel.

## What is a travel planning fee?

A planning fee is a charge the client pays the advisor directly for the work of designing a trip. It is usually collected up front, before research begins, and is often non-refundable because the work starts immediately.

Advisors structure planning fees in a few common ways:

| Model | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Flat fee per trip | One fee, set by trip type | Practices with predictable trip shapes |
| Tiered fee | Fee rises with complexity, length, or number of travelers | Multi-country and multi-generational trips |
| Hourly | Billed for time spent | Consulting or itinerary review work |
| Credited fee | Fee is applied toward the trip if the client books | Advisors moving from commission-only |

The credited fee is a common first step for advisors who have never charged. It establishes that the work has value without asking a long-time client to pay more than before.

## When does a retainer make sense?

A retainer is an annual or monthly fee that gives a client ongoing access: priority response, a set number of trips planned each year, holiday and peak-date monitoring, and the advisor's attention when something goes wrong mid-trip. It suits families and executives who travel often and value a single point of contact more than any individual booking.

Retainers are less common than planning fees and work best once a practice has clients who already behave like retainer clients: several trips a year, high expectations, and little interest in comparison shopping. Offering one to a first-time client is usually premature.

## How should an advisor present a planning fee?

State it plainly and early. A fee that first appears in the third email feels like a surprise. A fee that appears on your website, with a clear explanation of what it covers, reads as a sign of a serious practice.

Three things make the difference:

1. **Explain what the fee pays for.** Research, supplier negotiation, itinerary design, revisions, and support while the client is traveling. Clients accept a fee more easily when they can see the work.
2. **Tie it to outcomes, not hours.** The client is paying for the right villa, the room that faces the water, and someone to call when a flight cancels. Say that.
3. **Put it where prospects look.** Your website's "how it works" or "working together" page is where a referral checks whether you are the right fit. Advisors who publish their approach to fees tend to get fewer calls from people who were never going to pay one, and more from people who expected to.

## Does charging a fee cost you clients?

It changes which clients you get. Advisors who introduce planning fees often report fewer inquiries and better ones: clients who commit sooner, book what was designed for them, and refer people like themselves. The clients who leave over a fee were often the ones taking itineraries elsewhere.

The fee is easier to justify when everything around it matches the level of service. A prospect who finds a polished site that shows your hotel program access, your specialties, and a clear explanation of how you work is already halfway to accepting the fee before you discuss it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Do luxury travel advisors charge fees?**
Many do. Most luxury practices combine supplier commission with a planning fee charged directly to the client, and some offer annual retainers to frequent travelers. The fee pays for design work that commission does not cover.

**Is it more expensive to book through a travel advisor?**
Usually not for the booking itself. Hotels and cruise lines generally pay the advisor a commission, and clients typically pay the same rate as booking direct, often with added amenities through the advisor's consortium. A planning fee, if the advisor charges one, is separate and covers the design work.

**Are travel planning fees refundable?**
Most are not, because the work begins as soon as the fee is paid. Some advisors credit the fee toward the trip if the client books. Advisors should state their refund policy in writing before collecting the fee.

**How do I start charging a planning fee?**
Start with new clients, explain clearly what the fee covers, and consider crediting it toward the booking at first. Publish your approach on your website so prospects see it before the first call.

**What is a travel advisor retainer?**
An annual or monthly fee that gives a client ongoing access to the advisor: priority service, a set number of trips planned each year, and support while traveling. It suits clients who travel several times a year.
