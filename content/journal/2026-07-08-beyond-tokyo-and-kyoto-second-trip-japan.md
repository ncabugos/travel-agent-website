# A Second Trip to Japan: Beyond Tokyo and Kyoto

The first trip to Japan plans itself. Tokyo, then Kyoto, the bullet train between them, the temples everyone photographs. The second trip is the interesting one. **On a second trip to Japan, the move is to leave the Golden Route entirely and give your time to two or three regions most first-timers never reach — Kanazawa and the Hokuriku coast, the timber villages of the Japan Alps, and the art islands of the Seto Inland Sea.** These are not consolation prizes for having "done" the famous cities. For many travelers, they're the better Japan.

The reason is depth. The icons are crowded and time-boxed; the second-trip regions are slow, low-volume, and built around a single sustained experience — a craft, a bath, an island — rather than a checklist.

## Key Takeaways

- A second trip to Japan should skip re-doing Tokyo and Kyoto and commit to two or three less-trafficked regions instead — depth beats coverage.
- Kanazawa is the single best first step beyond the Golden Route: gold-leaf craft, a preserved samurai district, one of the country's great gardens, and a fraction of Kyoto's crowds.
- The Japan Alps — Takayama, the Kiso Valley, the village of Shirakawa-go — deliver the old rural Japan that the cities no longer show.
- The Setouchi region's art islands, anchored by Naoshima, pair celebrated contemporary-art museums with the calm of the Seto Inland Sea.
- The format that defines the second trip is the ryokan stay: a traditional inn where the cypress bath and the multi-course kaiseki dinner are the itinerary, not a break from it.
- As of 2026, the marquee ryokan and island hotels hold very few rooms and book six to twelve months out — this is a trip you plan early or not at all.

## When Should You Go?

The two best windows are autumn and late spring, and on a second trip the choice tilts toward November. The koyo — the turning of the maples — moves down the country through the month, and the gardens of Kanazawa and the valleys of the Japan Alps wear it better than the crowded Kyoto temples do. The air is crisp, the light is long, and the onsen towns are at their most appealing once there's a chill to come in from.

Late April into May is the other strong window, and it carries a specific draw: the spring Takayama Matsuri, one of Japan's most beautiful festivals, brings ornate floats through the Alps town in mid-April. The autumn Takayama Matsuri repeats the spectacle in October. Either is worth building a trip around — confirm the exact 2026 dates before you commit, since they anchor everything else.

Avoid the deep summer. July and August are hot and humid across most of Honshu, and the rainy season that precedes them can stall a tightly planned rural itinerary.

## Where to Base Yourself Beyond the Golden Route

Start with Kanazawa. It is the easiest leap from the familiar — a direct Shinkansen from Tokyo — and it pays off immediately. The Higashi Chaya district preserves the wooden teahouses and gold-leaf workshops that made the city wealthy; Kenroku-en is widely held to be one of the three great gardens of Japan, and in the off-season you can have stretches of it nearly to yourself. The city's food runs on the cold water of the Sea of Japan — the Omicho Market is the place to understand it, and *jibuni*, a duck-and-wheat-gluten stew, is the dish that belongs to Kanazawa specifically.

From there, the Japan Alps. Takayama is the hub, a town of dark-timber merchant houses and morning markets, and an hour or so on are the thatched-roof farmhouses of Shirakawa-go and the old post towns of the Kiso Valley, where the Nakasendo trail still connects Magome and Tsumago on foot. This is the rural Japan the cities have paved over.

The third region is Setouchi, the Seto Inland Sea. Naoshima and its neighbor islands turned a set of fishing communities into one of the world's most distinctive art destinations; Benesse House puts you on the island overnight, after the day-trippers have gone and the museums are quiet. The named ryokan that anchor each region — Beniya Mukayu on the Kaga coast, Amanemu down on the Ise-Shima peninsula — are worth orienting an itinerary around, though their rooms are few; confirm availability before you fix the dates.

## What's Worth Your Time

The single experience that defines the second trip is the ryokan night, and it deserves to be planned for rather than slotted in. A proper ryokan is not a hotel with a bath attached. The bath — often an *onsen* fed by a hot spring, sometimes a private cypress tub on your own terrace — and the *kaiseki* dinner served in your room or a quiet dining room are the entire point. Give it two nights, not one. The first night you learn the rhythm; the second you actually relax into it.

Beyond that, lean into the regional craft. In Kanazawa, that's gold leaf and Kutani porcelain. On Naoshima, it's the architecture itself — the way the museums are sunk into the hillsides to disappear. The thread is that each region rewards doing one thing deeply rather than five things briefly.

For travelers who want something the first trip couldn't offer, a night at a temple is the one to add. On Koyasan — the mountaintop monastic complex south of Osaka, the center of Shingon Buddhism — dozens of temples take overnight guests in a tradition called *shukubo*. You sleep in a working monastery, eat the vegetarian *shojin ryori* the monks prepare, and can join the morning prayers and the dawn walk through the Okunoin cemetery, where cedar trees and stone lanterns run for two kilometers to one of the country's most important sites. It is not a hotel experience dressed up as spirituality; it's the real thing, lightly opened to visitors. Pair it with the Kii Peninsula's pilgrimage trails if there's time, or take it on its own as a single, quiet night that reframes the whole trip.

## What to Skip on a Second Trip

Skip the instinct to re-anchor in Kyoto. The pull is understandable — it's the Japan you know — but spending the second trip's nights back in the crowded center of the Golden Route wastes the trip's whole premise. A night or two in the city for a specific reason is fine; basing there is a missed opportunity.

Skip the over-packed itinerary, too. The Japanese rail network makes it tempting to chain six regions in twelve days, and the result is a trip spent mostly in transit, watching the country through a train window. Two regions done slowly beats six done at speed every time. And skip the urge to schedule every ryokan day — the entire value of the format is the empty afternoon, the second soak, the walk with nowhere to be.

## How to Get Around

The rail network does the heavy lifting, and for a multi-region second trip it's almost always the right backbone. The Shinkansen reaches Kanazawa and the edge of the Alps directly; local lines and the occasional pre-arranged car handle the last stretch into the rural valleys and down to the ferry ports for Setouchi.

The one place a car earns its keep is the Alps, where the villages between Takayama and the Kiso Valley are spread thin and the bus timetables are sparse. As of 2026, seasonal timetable changes still catch travelers out on the rural lines, which is the unglamorous kind of detail worth handing to someone who tracks them.

The food is reason enough to plan the routing carefully. Each region beyond the Golden Route guards its own table: Hida beef in Takayama, marbled and grilled simply; the cold-water seafood and *jibuni* of Kanazawa; the udon and olive of the Setouchi islands. These aren't versions of Tokyo's dishes done at a distance — they're the local kitchens the cities draw from, eaten where they originate. Build a meal you've read about into each stop, and the itinerary half-plans itself around the table.

The second trip to Japan is less about where you go than how slowly you go there. Pick two regions, give each enough time to stop feeling like sightseeing, and the country opens up in a way the first trip only hinted at. If Japan is back on your list, the ryokan calendar is the part to start on first.
